    When the Land Breaks

    How ecocide, the mass destruction of ecosystems, pushes people toward traffickers, and how to stop the fall upstream.

    About 5 minutes · Based on Ecocide Meaning: How It Fuels Human Trafficking

    “Unlawful or wanton acts committed with knowledge that there is a substantial likelihood of severe and either widespread or long-term damage to the environment being caused by those acts.”

    The legal definition of ecocide · Independent Expert Panel, 2021

    What is ecocide?

    The large-scale destruction of ecosystems, so severe that people, species or whole ecosystems can no longer survive there.

    In 2021, an independent panel of lawyers convened by the Stop Ecocide Foundation, co-chaired by Philippe Sands and Dior Fall Sow, wrote its first legal definition. It covers unlawful or reckless acts, by people who knew the harm was likely.

    The damage has to be severe…

    …and either widespread or long-term.

    The aim is to make ecocide a fifth international crime, alongside genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes and aggression.

    The chain

    It starts with the land: forests cut, rivers poisoned, harvests failing.

    Livelihoods go with it: the farming, fishing and gathering that families depend on.

    People are uprooted, often with little or no compensation for what they lost.

    Far from home and without income, people take risks: debts, unsafe jobs, recruiters they do not know.

    Traffickers prey on that desperation.

    The last domino falls on people: forced labor and exploitation.

    So stop the fall upstream.

    Livelihoods that protect the land, and social enterprises like REBBL and Dignita, keep the first domino from taking the rest with it.

    The Amazon

    Logging and mining push deeper into indigenous land. People who lose it end up in illegal work, or exploited in cities.

    Southeast Asia

    Mangroves are cleared along the coasts. People who lose their homes and fishing grounds migrate, and many are forced into labor in cities.

    Sub-Saharan Africa

    Drought and land grabs push communities into cities, where trafficking networks are waiting.

    Madre de Dios, Peru

    Sometimes the chain also runs the other way. Not For Sale has documented people trapped in debt bondage being forced to clear forest and mine for gold: victims of trafficking, made to carry out ecocide.

    About this story

    One system, not two crises

    Ecocide destroys the land people live from. Displacement and desperation follow, and traffickers exploit them. Not For Sale works on both at once, because confronting one without the other leaves both intact.
