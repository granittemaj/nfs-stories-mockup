    Slavery Changes Shape

    From the first civilizations to the scam compounds of today: the long history of slavery, and of the fight against it.

    About 7 minutes · Based on The History of Modern-Day Slavery by Mark Wexler

    1. The ancient world

      In Mesopotamia and Egypt, enslaved people worked in homes, in fields and on great building projects. In Athens they rowed galleys and worked the mines. Rome held vast numbers, from servants and artisans to gladiators.

    2. From the 7th century

      In the Islamic world, enslaved people served as soldiers, such as the Mamluks of Egypt, and as servants and laborers. A trade carried people from Africa to the Middle East and North Africa.

    3. Across Africa

      Many forms of servitude existed. Some were close to Europe’s serfdom; others enslaved prisoners of war, or people in debt.

    4. Across Asia

      In China, slavery was mostly domestic. In Southeast Asia it ranged from war captives to debt bondage.

    5. The Americas, before 1492

      The Aztecs and the Maya held slaves for labor, for sacrifice, and as “pawns” held against a debt.

    6. The Middle Ages

      Across Europe, serfdom bound people to the land, and to the will of the people who owned it.

    7. These forms of slavery were often based on status, debt, war or religion. Not on race.

      That was about to change.

    1500

    From around 1500

    European nations such as Portugal captured and enslaved African people and shipped them across the Atlantic.

    Most were taken to Brazil and the Caribbean, to work on plantations.

    Others were taken to the Spanish Americas and North America. Ships carried sugar and cotton back to Europe.

    12.5 million

    people were forced onto slave ships over more than three centuries. About 10.7 million survived the crossing.

    Source: SlaveVoyages, Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database

    The trade was legal, endorsed and regulated by governments. And unlike most slavery before it, it was built on race.

    1807

    After years of campaigning by William Wilberforce and others, Britain’s Slave Trade Act ended its part in the trade.

    1866

    The transatlantic trade finally ended. Slavery did not.

    1. 1759 to 1833 · William Wilberforce

      The British politician campaigned for decades against the trade. His work helped pass the 1807 Slave Trade Act, and inspired movements in other countries.

    2. The 1800s · The United States

      The California Gold Rush and the Transcontinental Railroad drew Chinese immigrants. Many men were trafficked for labor in harsh conditions; women were coerced into prostitution by criminal syndicates known as Tongs.

    3. And so, slowly, the world began to write laws against it.

    1904

    1904 and 1910

    The first international agreements against trafficking. Their language reflected the prejudices of their time.

    1919 to 1930

    The International Labour Organization is founded. A League of Nations convention covers trafficking in women and children. Then the ILO defines forced labor: work demanded under threat of a penalty, which a person has not offered freely.

    1949

    The United Nations adopts its first legally binding agreement on human trafficking.

    2000

    The Palermo Protocol gives the world its first agreed definition of human trafficking. The United States passes the Trafficking Victims Protection Act.

    2011 to 2024

    The EU Anti-Trafficking Directive, the UK Modern Slavery Act, and in 2024 an EU update that adds forced marriage, illegal adoption and surrogacy.

    • 28 million in forced labor
    • 22 million in forced marriage

    Today, an estimated 50 million people live in modern slavery. Each square here stands for 100,000 people.

    About 28 million are in forced labor.

    About 22 million are in forced marriage.

    About these numbers

    They are estimates for 2021 by the ILO, Walk Free and the IOM, published in 2022. They are not a new count for 2026.

    Online

    Traffickers recruit through false job adverts, grooming and threats. Contact online turns into exploitation offline.

    This is how The Offer begins

    Forced criminality

    Some people are trafficked into compounds and made to run online scams. The person sending a scam message may be under threat themselves.

    Climate change

    Droughts, floods and failing harvests push people from their homes and livelihoods. Traffickers exploit their desperation.

    About this story

    Based on our history of slavery

    This story is drawn from The History of Modern-Day Slavery and Human Trafficking by Mark Wexler. The full article goes deeper into each era, with further reading, documentaries and the laws of individual countries.

    Modern-day slavery and human trafficking are connected, but not the same. Slavery describes exploitation a person cannot refuse or leave. Trafficking is the recruitment, movement or harboring of people for exploitation, and it does not need to cross a border.
