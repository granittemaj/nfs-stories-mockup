    The Offer

    How a job offer becomes a trap, and how the way out begins. Follow Ana’s phone.

    About 6 minutes · October 2026

    This story describes forced labor. Ana is a composite, drawn from patterns our team in the Netherlands sees again and again. No real person is shown.

    Ana is 19. The shoe factory where she worked closed in the spring. Nothing has opened since.

    Her mother cleans offices at night. The bills on the table don’t wait.

    The offer came from Mihai, a friend of a cousin. That is how it usually works: someone you know, or someone who knows someone.

    What we know

    A false job offer is one of the most common ways people are drawn into trafficking.

    Source: UNODC, Global Report on Trafficking in Persons

    The restaurant is real. So are the guests on the ground floor.

    Below them, the kitchen runs 14 hours a day, seven days a week.

    Upstairs, six women share one room. At night, the door is locked from the outside.

    On the second day, the manager takes Ana’s phone. “So you can focus,” he says.

    Then he opens his book. The bus ride was not free after all.

    Her bed costs €350 a month. Food, €150. Then there is an “agency fee”.

    Every broken plate is a fine. After three months, Ana “owes” €2,800. She has been paid €150.

    What we know

    This is debt bondage: a debt that can never be paid off, used to keep someone working. Our team in the Netherlands sees it again and again, along with taken documents and threats.

    In March, labor inspectors walk into the kitchen. A guest had called.

    Ana is offered a safe place to stay, and time to think about what comes next.

    Getting out is the first step. Building a life after it takes much longer.

    In Amsterdam, Not For Sale runs Dignita: a restaurant where every euro of profit goes back into training survivors.

    The Dignita Academy, built with ROC van Amsterdam, gives survivors accredited training in the kitchen, at the coffee bar, in beauty and in administration.

    Trainees cook for real guests from day one. Their work is on the menu.

    Ana trains as a barista. Eight months later, her phone lights up again.

    Another job offer. This time, there is a contract.

    Marloes, a café manager: “We’d like to offer you a barista job from June. 32 hours a week.” A contract is attached.

    This time, the offer is real.

    Ana replies: “Yes. Thank you!” Then a message from her mother: “I’m so proud of you.”

    About this story

    Ana is not one person

    Ana’s story is a composite. It is drawn from patterns that Not For Sale’s team in the Netherlands sees again and again: a job offer from someone you half know, documents taken on the way, a debt that keeps growing, a door that locks. Names and details are changed, and no real person is shown.

    Not For Sale began its work in Amsterdam in 2012 with Home Soup, a soup-making program inside a safe house. In 2015 it became Dignita, a restaurant that trains survivors for real jobs and pays for that training with its own profits.
